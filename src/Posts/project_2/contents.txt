What is a pulse oximeter?
A key part of year 1 was developing the understanding of the principles of op-amps. In year 2, we got to blend it with medical photonics technology in a pulse oximeter group project. A pulse oximeter measures heart rate and blood oxygen levels using red and infrared frequencies of light. Oxygenated hemoglobin absorbs more IR light whilst deoxygenated hemoglobin absorbs more red light. Though theoretically red LEDs can also be used to measure heart rate, the peak is significantly less pronounced that IR so when amplifying the current signal red LEDs will need a higher gain and thus increasing the effect of noise distortion. 

What was our design?
So the preliminary design uses fast switching red and IR LEDs through a probe, to which it is read using a photodiode similar to the IR LED circuit we used in the year 1 EEE bot. However, this time the LEDs were toggled in a 50% duty cycle PWM signal through BJTs and an STM32 microcontroller's PWM output. Then the small current signal from the photodiode is run through a transimpedence amplifier, providing it enough DC offset for noticable peaks in the 0-3.3V ADC range of the STM32 and then 512 samples are used in a fast fourier transform (FFT) to provide the frequency of our heart beat. To calculate blood oxygen levels, we used the ratio of the red to IR reading to their respective DC offsets whilst scaling it by comparing the reading to an actual pulse oximeter reading. We then displayed the readings on a mini OLED screen using I2C from the STM32 whilst reading from serial in Putty using USART, however in doing so we occupied STM32s serial port and we weren't able to plot data using the STM32CubeIDE's variable plotter. To fix this, we toggled USART output in code such that we could turn on and off the plotter for demonstrations.

The 7-segment display
In addition to photonics, the project was also used as an introduction to CPLDs where we used Intel Quartus Primes to simulate a UART bus (connected through a coaxial cable) and control 2 7-segment LEDs. However, the final logic gate circuit didn't work due to an issue I later debugged where the CPLD wouldn't lock in the value for the 7-segment and instead clear (set to 0) straight after displaying the number input. Since the CPLD was operating at 100kHz using an external crystal oscillator, the successful toggling of the display wasn't noticable due to how fast it switched which made debugging significantly more time consuming so my teammate suggested switching to verilog in the end for the demonstration due to the tight time constraint.

Improvements
One thing we noticed when trying to find the gain of the transimpedence amplifier was that the differential IR absorbtion of different skin tones meant that the device was only compatible to one skin tone. Whilst one of our group members could have a perfect PPG trace from the pulse oximeter, another could be flat lined, so the results vary across members. One way to resolve this is to have a separate feedback loop which controls the gain of the transimpedence amp with digital potentiometers to ensure consistency. Another issue was the accuracy of the heart rate. The fast fourier transform didnt have a precise enough frequency output to account for such a slow heart rate compared to the internal sample rate of the ADC, and the result has to be taken over a small sample size and scaled by 60 for beats per minute so any minor errors in accuracy are amplified and our final reading had an accuracy of +-4bpm which is abysmal. So, next time we need to accumilate more samples over time to apply a FFT to in order to increase accuracy which would result in significantly longer settling times for the pulse oximeter but seeing as its medical tech, I'd image accuracy to be the priority so as to not misdiagnose a patient.

Aside from these severe design flaws, there are a lot of other considerations like power consumption and the fact that the design was significantly larger that commercial probes. Since we only used the STM32 for fast fourier transforms, PWM, I2C and USART, we could switch over to smaller ESP32s like the ESP32 Cx Mini series and condition the ADCs to provide the same results but smaller, at a far lower power draw.
